Fansign video call FAQ

Everything fans ask
before the call.

Real answers from fans who've been there. What to say in Korean, how to handle the nerves, what happens on the day — and how to walk away without wishing.

I just won a video call fansign and I'm panicking. What do I do first?

First — congratulations. And yes, the panic is completely normal. Almost every winner describes the same feeling: excitement for about ten seconds, then "wait, what do I actually say?"

Here's a simple order that works: (1) Check your event details — date, time zone, and the app the call will use. (2) Decide on 2–3 things you genuinely want to say, not ten. (3) Practice saying them out loud before the day comes. That last step is the one most fans skip — and the one that changes everything.

I barely speak Korean. Can I still do a fancall?

Yes. At overseas fansign events, fans who don't speak Korean are the majority, not the exception — one fan who attended a European fansign put it simply: "most winners didn't speak Korean, we were all super nervous as well."

Idols know this. They're used to it. A short, genuine sentence in simple Korean lands better than a long perfect one — and smiling through a mistake is a completely valid strategy. You need a few lines you're comfortable with, not a whole language.

What should I actually say during my 90 seconds?

The structure that works for most fans: a greeting, one main thing you came to say, one short question or reaction, and a goodbye. That's it — four beats.

Keep each line short enough to say in one breath. "오늘 너무 행복해요" (I'm so happy today) beats a three-clause sentence you'll trip over. And prepare one flexible reaction like "진짜요?" (really?) for whatever they say back — the call is a conversation, not a speech.

If you want to rehearse the whole 90 seconds — including how you'll react when they answer — that's exactly what we built our practice call for.

I'm scared I'll freeze the moment the call connects. How do I prepare for that?

Freezing doesn't mean you're unprepared — it usually means you care a lot. Fans who've done multiple calls say the nerves never fully disappear; they just get more manageable each time.

Two things help most: (1) practicing out loud, not in your head — your mouth needs the rehearsal more than your brain does, and (2) having your first line so ready that it comes out automatically. Once the first sentence lands, the rest tends to follow.

Is it okay to read from a script?

You can, but there are two honest downsides. Machine-translated scripts often sound unnatural — fans have described rewriting a friend's Google-translated script minutes before a call because it read so awkwardly. And reading while on camera splits your attention, so you miss the moment you paid for.

A better middle ground: write your lines, then practice them until you only need a one-word memo per line. You keep the safety net without spending your 90 seconds looking down.

How long is a fancall, really? It feels like it ends instantly.

Most video call events give you somewhere between 60 seconds and 3 minutes, depending on how many items you purchased and the event's rules. And yes — whatever the number is, it will feel shorter.

The fans who walk away satisfied usually aren't the ones who said the most. They're the ones who decided in advance what mattered most and said that first. Front-load what you came to say; treat everything after as a bonus.

What if I don't understand what my idol says back?

This happens constantly, and it's okay. A few fans' favorite recovery lines: "다시 한번 말해주세요" (please say that once more), or simply reacting with warmth — a laugh, "진짜요?", a hand on your heart — keeps the moment alive even when the words don't land.

One thing to know in advance: staff usually won't translate mid-call. So it helps to practice hearing simple Korean, not just saying it. Listening is half the call.

What happens if the call drops or the connection is bad?

Technical issues are more common than you'd think — frozen screens, audio delays, calls cutting out. If it happens: don't panic and don't hang up. Message the event staff immediately (keep that chat window ready before the call). Many events will reconnect you or adjust your time.

Before the day: use Wi-Fi you trust, close every other app, and do one test call with a friend on the same app if you can.

What's the waiting process like before the call? I heard there's a test call.

Most events run a test call (checking your camera, audio, and identity) before the real one — often 30–60 minutes earlier. Then you wait for staff to call you when it's your turn.

Fair warning from fans who've been through it: the waiting room is often the most stressful part — some say it's worse than the call itself. Have water nearby, keep your notes in view, and remember the hard part ends the second you see their face.

Can I record my fancall?

It depends entirely on the event. Some officially provide a recording afterward, some allow self-recording, and some prohibit it. Check your event notice — and if it's unclear, ask staff during the test call.

If recording is allowed, set it up before the waiting room, not during. More than a few fans have lost their only copy of the call to a recording button that never got pressed.

How do I know if a winner notification is real or a scam?

Sadly, fake "winner" messages exist. Real notifications come from the platform you actually purchased on, reference your real order, and never ask you to download an unfamiliar app or send a photo of your passport/ID through chat to "verify" you before the event.

Red flags fans have reported: unexpected contact through WeChat or personal DMs, urgent "confirm within hours" pressure, and ID photo requests. When in doubt, contact the store's official support directly — never through the link in the suspicious message.

Do idols mind that overseas fans can't speak Korean?

By every account we've seen — no. The opposite, actually. One idol shared on a livestream that when overseas fans try hard to deliver their prepared Korean and run out of time, he finds it so touching that he asks staff to cover the camera after the call ends.

They see the effort, not the accuracy. Your 90 seconds doesn't need to be perfect Korean. It needs to be yours.
